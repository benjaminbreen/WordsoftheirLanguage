## 1. "Certaine words of Pegu language" (A true and large discourse..., 1603; EEBO A21094)

**Verdict: Known.** Mon was identified in 1940.

- **Sir William Foster, ed., *The Voyages of Sir James Lancaster to Brazil and the East Indies, 1591-1603* (Hakluyt Society, 2nd ser. 85, 1940), p. 141, n. 3.** Foster reprints the list and notes that Dr [C. O.] Blagden examined it and found "the language is Mon (Talaing)", with most words recognizable. Full text: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.61543 (the list and note are about line 9039 of the djvu text).
  - Blagden was the leading Mon epigraphist of his day, so this is an expert identification. It is not a passing guess.
- **Michael W. Charney, ed., "Certaine Words of Pegu Language (1603)", *SOAS Bulletin of Burma Research* 2.2 (Autumn 2004): 201-202.** This is a transcription with an editor's note.
  - Repository: https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/431097
  - Copy: https://archive.org/details/CertaineWordsOfPeguLanguage1603
  - Charney notes that the fleet "does not appear to have visited Pegu". He says nothing in the text shows how or where the list was obtained, and suggests it may have come from Peguan traders met at sea, in the Straits of Melaka, or in a port.
  - He does not analyse the language. The archive.org metadata tags it "Mon language; Peguan".
- **Compiler and place:** no published attribution was found. The list is printed after the homeward journal of the *Dragon* (Lancaster's flagship), so it was probably collected by someone aboard. Peguan/Mon traders and slaves were present at Aceh and Bantam, which is a plausible source. This remains an inference; Charney leaves it open.
- **Earliest attestation?** Neither Foster nor Charney claims it is the earliest European record of Mon. I found no earlier printed European Mon word list, so it may well be the earliest printed one, but **I could not confirm this**. Fitch, Balbi and Federici describe Pegu but give no vocabularies.
- **What could still be new:**
  - A modern item-by-item etymological analysis against Shorto's Mon dictionaries. Blagden's identification was a one-line footnote, and Charney did not analyse the words.
  - Analysis of the possibly non-Mon items, e.g. *Tobacu* (pen), *Cabang* (ship; cf. Malay *kapal*?), *Nepoe*, *True*.
  - Dialect or period features (Middle Mon), and confirmation by blind computational methods.
  - The basic identification "Mon, not Burmese" is not new.

## 2. Richard Boothby, *A Briefe Discovery or Description of ... Madagascar* (1646; EEBO A28809)

**Verdict: Partly known.** The link to St Augustine's Bay and a southwestern dialect is stated. No scholarly dialect analysis was found.

- **Wikipedia, "Documentation of the Malagasy language".** It has a section "Richard Boothby's records from Saint Augustine Bay (1646)" with a 19-item comparison table.
  - URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Documentation_of_the_Malagasy_language
  - It asserts the language "is now classified as part of the Vezo dialect ... likely representing an early form of Proto-Vezo".
  - Its only citation is Boothby himself (pp. 19-20). The classification looks like an editor's inference from the location, not a published analysis.
- **Boothby's own visit:** he was at St Augustine's Bay in **1630**, on his way home from India. This is stated in IRD-hosted scholarship on Peter Mundy, which notes that the name Andropela had been "already encountered by Boothby in 1630": https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_5/b_fdi_10-11/12781.pdf. Because of this, the list predates the Courteen colony (1645-46) and was not collected by it.
- **Link to the Courteen colony:** the link is through promotion, not collection.
  - Edmond Smith, "'Canaanising Madagascar': Africa in English imperial imagination, 1635-1650", *Itinerario* (2018). OA copy: https://kar.kent.ac.uk/60175/
  - Smith shows that Boothby's text circulated in manuscript before the 1645 colony and shaped it. It was printed in 1646, after the colony failed.
  - Powle Waldegrave, a survivor, attacked Boothby for "Canaanising" Madagascar.
- **Earlier southwestern data:** François Martin de Vitré recorded numerals at St Augustine's Bay in 1603 (*Description du premier voyage*, 1604, p. 69). Wikipedia labels these Old Vezo or Masikoro. Boothby is therefore not the earliest record of southwestern Malagasy. He may be the earliest English-language Malagasy word list, but I could not confirm that against Lancaster-era English voyage texts, some of which also contain Malagasy words. For example, Foster 1940 (p. 127, n.) notes Malagasy words in a Lancaster-voyage text.
- **Not found:** any analysis by Gueunier, Larson, Adelaar, Beaujard or Ferrand that assigns Boothby's words to a dialect. Adelaar's recent Houtman work is on the 1603 northeastern list: https://scholarhub.ui.ac.id/wacana/vol25/iss3/4/.
- **What could be new:**
  - A systematic, quantitative placement of the list. Our result (Antaisaka/Tanosy/Bara closest) differs in label from Wikipedia's "Vezo". The difference may be a sampling artefact, because ASJP/Lexibank may lack Vezo and Masikoro doculects. Check this before claiming a disagreement.
  - Showing that the forms are southern/southwestern on *linguistic* grounds, not just from the place of collection.

## 3. Robert Dudley's Trinidad list (1595; Hakluyt, *Principal Navigations* III, 1600)

**Verdict: Known**, including the da- "my" prefix.

- **George F. Warner, ed., *The Voyage of Robert Dudley ... to the West Indies, 1594-1595* (Hakluyt Society, 2nd ser. 3, 1899).**
  - Full text: https://archive.org/details/voyageofrobertdu00warnrich
  - Introduction: Dudley and Captain Wyatt both "give lists of words in their language, from which it is evident that they were of the well-known Arawak tribe". The vocabularies "appear to be the earliest on record".
  - Notes on Wyatt's list (about p. 65): the language is Arawak, compared with the 1598 Cabeliau list (printed in De Laet 1633), Brinton (1871) and Sagot (1882). Warner explicitly states that the prefixes "wa and da respectively mean 'our' and 'my'".
  - Note on Dudley's list (about p. 78): further Arawak equivalents, e.g. *katti* moon, *simara* arrow, *kalli* cassava.
- **Arie Boomert, *The Indigenous Peoples of Trinidad and Tobago from the First Settlers until Today* (Sidestone, 2016), p. 100.** Open access: https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789088903533.pdf. Boomert says Dudley at Curiapan was "trading with the Arawaks (from whom he collected word lists)". He also notes that Cabeliau in 1598 collected separate Arawak, Yaio and Shebaio lists.
- **No competing attribution** to Nepoya, Yao or Carib was found.
- **Note:** Hakluyt prints *two* Trinidad lists. One is Dudley's own "certaine wordes of the language of Trinidad"; the other is in Wyatt's narrative. Check which one our pipeline ingested.
- **What could be new:** only the method (blind computational confirmation) and finer points, such as whether any items are Cariban or Shebaio loans. The identification itself is 125+ years old.

## 4. Stephen Burrough's Kola list (1557; Hakluyt 1589)

**Verdict: Known.** It is the recognised earliest Saami word list, and its dialect has been identified.

- **John Abercromby, "The earliest list of Russian Lapp words", *Journal de la Société Finno-Ougrienne / SUSA* 13.2 (1895): 1-8.**
  - Full text: https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_46IAAAAAYAAJ
  - He edits all of the roughly 95 items and compares them with Ter forms from Genetz's Kola Lapp dictionary.
  - He concludes that Burrough's informants were not ancestors of the Jokonga (Ter) Lapps and "must have come from further west or south west".
- **Arvid Genetz, "Bemerkungen zum Obigen", same issue, pp. 8-10.** Genetz finds "a decided agreement with the present Kildin (or Semiostrov) dialect", deviating "just as decidedly from Ter". He gives a sound-correspondence table.
  - He also suggests that *nenna*, *nealma* and *pannea* may be possessive forms ("my nose", "my mouth", "my tooth").
  - He concludes that the dialect has stayed largely unchanged for 300 years.
- **Modern restatements:**
  - Michael Rießler, "Kola Sámi literature" (2018): the list is "the first written record of a Sámi language", in "a dialect of either Ter Sámi or Kildin Sámi". OA: http://hdl.handle.net/10138/305133
  - Wikipedia "Stephen Borough" calls it 95 Kildin Sami words and the earliest documentation of Sami: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Borough
- **What could be new:** only the method, and possibly the Skolt/Kildin closeness, since Genetz contrasted Kildin with Ter only. Our "Eastern Saami, Kildin closest" result agrees with Genetz 1895.

## 5. Thomas Herbert, "The Malayan Tongue" (1634; 1638), and van Neck's journal (London, 1601)

**Verdict: Apparently new**, with the caveats below.

What I checked:
- **Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Herbert, Thomas":** https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/herbert-thomas-2/. It discusses Herbert's reading of Knolles, Bedwell and Brerewood. It says nothing about his vocabularies or about van Neck.
- **Margaret Maclean, Univ. of Leicester Special Collections blog:** https://staffblogs.le.ac.uk/specialcollections/2015/01/07/17th-century-adventures-in-travel-writing/. It does not mention the Malay list. Some secondary descriptions present Herbert's "quick dictionary of Malayan" as his own mercantile glossary and do not mention a source.
- **British Library "Early vocabularies of Malay"** (Annabel Gallop's blog, 2015; mirror at https://librarylearningspace.com/early-vocabularies-malay/). It covers Houtman 1603, Spalding 1614 and Bowrey 1701, but neither Herbert nor van Neck.
- **Tom G. Hoogervorst, "Seventeenth-century Malay wordlists and their potential for etymological scholarship", *Wacana* 25.3 (2024).**
  - URL: https://scholarhub.ui.ac.id/wacana/vol25/iss3/7/
  - It compares VOC-era Dutch sources and cites Keuning's edition of the van Neck voyage. Its landing page shows no mention of Herbert.
  - The full PDF was blocked (403), so I could not check whether it notes later English reuse.
- **Not accessible:**
  - LEME's entry for Herbert, if one exists (the site returned 403/404).
  - John Anthony Butler's annotated editions of Herbert (ACMRS, 2012 and later). Butler annotates Herbert's borrowings, so a source note at the Malay list is possible.
- **Context:**
  - The Malay list in the 1601 English van Neck journal (trans. William Walker) is a translation of the vocabulary appended to the Dutch *Het tweede boeck* (1601) of the 1598-1600 voyage. So Herbert's ultimate source is the Dutch second-voyage vocabulary.
  - Its glosses were shuffled through English, which explains errors like Gréene/*Ise* → "I see".
  - It is not Houtman 1603 or Spalding 1614. Houtman's is a separate work with dialogues.
- **Assessment:** I found no published statement that Herbert copied his Malay list from van Neck (English 1601 or Dutch). I also found none on the re-glossing errors. Given Herbert's known heavy use of printed sources, the dependency might be noted in a footnote in Butler's edition.
  - **Before claiming novelty, check Butler's edition at the Malay list, and Hoogervorst 2024's full text.**
  - The specific error analysis (e.g. "Faire - Apon" → "A shooe"; "NO - Tieda" → "To spin") is very likely new even if the copying itself has been noted.

### Caveats
- Web search under-covers print-only scholarship. For example, I could not check Douglas Taylor's *Languages of the West Indies* (1977), Gueunier's articles on old Malagasy wordlists, or Bauer's Mon studies at page level. None of these would overturn the "Known" verdicts above, which rest on explicit primary statements.
